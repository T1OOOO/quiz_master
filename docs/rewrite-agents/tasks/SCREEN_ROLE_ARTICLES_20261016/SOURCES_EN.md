## 015 — *Breakfast at Tiffany’s* (1961): Audrey Hepburn — Holly Golightly

1. [AFI Catalog: *Breakfast at Tiffany’s* (1961)](https://catalog.afi.com/Film/22674-BREAKFAST-ATTIFFANYS) — AFI credits Audrey Hepburn as Holly Golightly and identifies the film as Paramount’s adaptation of Truman Capote’s 1958 novella. The production history records that other performers, including Marilyn Monroe, were considered; that is casting history, not a reason to blur the finished-film credit.
2. [Academy: The 34th Academy Awards (1962)](https://www.oscars.org/oscars/ceremonies/1962) — the ceremony records five nominations and two wins: Henry Mancini’s dramatic/comedy score and “Moon River” (Mancini music, Johnny Mercer lyrics). It also lists Hepburn’s Actress nomination and George Axelrod’s adapted-screenplay nomination. The 1962 ceremony recognizes 1961 releases.

**Safe article material:** introduce Holly as a fictional screen character, connect the finished credit to the Capote adaptation, then explain how the awards page separates the score/song wins from Hepburn’s acting nomination. Do not state that Monroe played Holly or that Hepburn won an Oscar for this performance.

## 016 — *Taxi Driver* (1976): Robert De Niro — Travis Bickle

1. [AFI Catalog: *Taxi Driver* (1976)](https://catalog.afi.com/Film/67969-) — AFI’s cast record credits Robert De Niro as Travis Bickle. Its synopsis starts with the 26-year-old veteran applying to drive a New York taxicab; its production history says De Niro drove a taxi while preparing and says the mirror line was improvised. AFI also records the 1976 Palme d’Or and four Academy nominations.
2. [Academy: The 49th Academy Awards (1977)](https://www.oscars.org/oscars/ceremonies/1977/A) — the Academy page says it honors 1976 releases and lists *Taxi Driver* among Best Picture nominees, Bernard Herrmann’s score among nominees, and Jodie Foster among supporting-actress nominees. It is useful to distinguish nominations from wins.

**Safe article material:** call Travis a fictional character and frame the story as a New York-set film narrative, not a factual account of a real cab driver. Use the AFI credit for the answer and the documented preparation/improvisation as craft context; phrase the latter as AFI-reported production history, not an unsupported anecdote.

## 017 — *All About Eve* (1950): Bette Davis — Margo Channing

1. [AFI Catalog: *All About Eve* (1950)](https://catalog.afi.com/Catalog/MovieDetails/26204) — AFI credits Bette Davis as Margo Channing and documents intermittent voice-over narration from Margo, Addison DeWitt and Karen Richards. It reports that Mankiewicz connected Margo’s archetype to the eighteenth-century actor Peg Woffington, while explicitly presenting this as interview-derived production context.
2. [Academy: The 23rd Academy Awards (1951)](https://www.oscars.org/oscars/ceremonies/1951) — the Academy lists *All about Eve* as Best Motion Picture winner, Joseph L. Mankiewicz as Directing winner, and Edith Head and Charles LeMaire as winners for black-and-white costume design. The 1951 ceremony honors 1950 releases.

**Safe article material:** identify Margo as a fictional stage star and Davis as the credited actor; discuss the film’s narration structure and awards as craft/history context. The Peg Woffington note is an interpretive origin for an archetype, not evidence that Margo is a historical person or a biography of Woffington.

## 018 — *On the Waterfront* (1954): Marlon Brando — Terry Malloy

1. [AFI Catalog: *On the Waterfront* (1954)](https://catalog.afi.com/Film/51286-on-thewaterfront) — AFI credits Marlon Brando as Terry Malloy and describes him in the synopsis as a former boxer working on the docks. It says Budd Schulberg based story and screenplay on Malcolm Johnson’s 1948 *New York Sun* series about longshoremen and union corruption, then conducted further waterfront research.
2. [Academy: *On the Waterfront*](https://www.oscars.org/events/waterfront) — the Academy describes Brando’s Terry as a former prizefighter and longshoreman, credits Kazan, Schulberg, Bernstein, Kaufman and Day, and says the film was nominated for 12 Oscars and won eight, including Actor for Brando and Directing for Kazan. It also notes the Hudson River location shooting and a restoration credit.

**Safe article material:** distinguish the fictional Malloy and plot from Johnson’s real investigative reporting. The source connection supports discussion of inspiration and research, not a claim that the film recounts one documented person’s life.
